The one fact to remember tonight

Every preventive measure removes paths of attack, and every preventive measure is also a change that can break something. Choose the measures that reach the required level with independent layers, then assess and manage what each measure introduces.

From treatment decisions to preventive measures

In Classes 12 and 13 you decided how to treat Kalmia's twelve risks and you compared the cost of the controls with the losses they avoid. Tonight we study the part of the plan that acts before an attack succeeds: the preventive measures. A preventive measure is a control whose purpose is to make a threat event less likely; it acts before the event and therefore lowers mainly the likelihood of the risk. The course outline names five families of preventive measures — hardening, patch management, access control, segmentation and awareness — and two questions that decide whether a plan is good: does it reach the required level of security and resilience with independent layers, and which new risks do the measures themselves introduce? One lesson for each family, then one lesson for each question.

The attack surface

Every preventive measure of this hour works on one quantity: the attack surface. The attack surface of a system is the set of points through which an attacker can try to enter it, change it or extract data from it: every network service that listens on a port, every account and password, every installed program, every physical interface such as a USB (Universal Serial Bus) port, every web form. A larger surface means more possible paths. Removing a path that the business does not need removes, at the same time, every vulnerability on that path — including the vulnerabilities nobody has discovered yet. This is why the first preventive measure is subtraction, not addition.

System hardening

System hardening is the configuration work that reduces the attack surface of a system. The NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) glossary defines hardening as a process intended to eliminate a means of attack by patching vulnerabilities and turning off nonessential services. In practice, hardening acts on six areas:

  • Accounts: change every default password, disable the built-in Guest account and unused accounts, keep only approved members in the local Administrators group (a unique local administrator password per computer, managed by Windows LAPS [Local Administrator Password Solution]).
  • Services and ports: stop the services the business does not use; replace clear-text administration (Telnet, HTTP [Hypertext Transfer Protocol]) with encrypted administration (SSH [Secure Shell], HTTPS [Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure]).
  • Protocols: turn off obsolete protocols such as SMBv1 (version 1 of the Server Message Block protocol), the file-sharing protocol that the WannaCry worm used in 2017.
  • Host protections: host firewall on every network profile, anti-malware real-time protection, application allow lists.
  • Data protection: full-disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows), above all on laptops.
  • Session settings: automatic screen lock, automatic logoff, no automatic execution of USB (Universal Serial Bus) content.

Hardening is not improvised from memory. It follows a secure configuration baseline: a documented list of settings that every system of one type must have, each with its required value. Organizations adapt published baselines: the CIS (Center for Internet Security) Benchmarks of the Center for Internet Security, the security baselines that Microsoft publishes with its Security Compliance Toolkit, and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's guidance Harden operating systems and applications (ITSM.10.090), action no. 4 of its Top 10 IT (information technology) security actions. A baseline is used twice: when a system is installed, to configure it, and later, to audit it. The audit detects configuration drift — the gradual return of insecure settings after repairs, quick fixes and software updates.

Kalmia already gave us examples in Class 6: Telnet and factory passwords on cameras CAM-HQ-3 and CAM-HQ-4, the administration page of NAS-01 reachable over HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol) from every device, the community string 'public' on printer PRN-HQ-1, and a Wi-Fi extender with the password admin/admin. Each one is a hardening finding. Tonight's example adds the Windows computers.

Patch management

A patch, or security update, is a piece of software published by a vendor to correct a defect, often a vulnerability. Patch management is the organized process of identifying, obtaining, testing, installing and verifying patches. NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-40 Rev. 4 — Guide to Enterprise Patch Management Planning: Preventive Maintenance for Technology (April 2022) presents it as preventive maintenance for technology: planned, routine and documented, like the scheduled maintenance of the roaster, not an emergency reaction. The process has seven steps:

  1. Inventory — you can only patch what you know you own (Lab 1).
  2. Monitor the sources — vendor bulletins, the CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, United States) Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV [Known Exploited Vulnerabilities]) Catalog, the alerts of the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security.
  3. Assess and prioritize — severity (CVSS [Common Vulnerability Scoring System]), evidence of exploitation (KEV [Known Exploited Vulnerabilities], EPSS [Exploit Prediction Scoring System]), exposure to the Internet and criticality of the asset: the priority score of Class 6.
  4. Test — install first on a pilot ring, a small group of representative computers.
  5. Deploy — in a maintenance window, ring after ring.
  6. Verify — check that the update is really installed and the service still works.
  7. Report — status per asset, exceptions and their end dates.

Every step needs an owner and every patch needs a deadline. Kalmia's patch policy, control CT-32 proposed tonight, uses the priority classes of Class 12: P1 within 7 days (for example, a vulnerability listed in the KEV [Known Exploited Vulnerabilities] catalog on a system reachable from the Internet), P2 within 30 days, P3 within 90 days. For comparison, CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, United States)'s Binding Operational Directive 22-01 requires United States federal agencies to remediate newly listed KEV vulnerabilities within two weeks.

Two situations break the routine. A zero-day vulnerability is known to attackers before a patch exists: the organization applies a workaround or a compensating control — a control that reduces the same risk when the normal control cannot be applied. An end-of-support product (also called end of life) will never receive a patch again: Windows 10 since October 14, 2025; Windows 11 version 24H2 Home and Pro since October 2026 (Microsoft release information); PHP (PHP: Hypertext Preprocessor, a web programming language) 8.1 since December 31, 2025 (PHP supported versions). For such a product there are only three options: upgrade it, replace it, or isolate it with compensating controls while its replacement is prepared.

One historical case shows why deadlines matter. Microsoft published the update MS17-010 for SMBv1 (version 1 of the Server Message Block protocol) on March 14, 2017. In May 2017, the WannaCry worm used that same vulnerability and reached more than 200,000 computers within a few days — computers that had missed an update available for two months. MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge) lists the same practice as mitigation M1051 Update Software.

Kalmia: who patches what?

Class 9 recorded risk factor KRF-20: NAS-01, the cameras and ROAST-PC have no patch owner. InfoNord's contract covers 'the server', the office PCs (personal computers) update themselves, and everything else depends on someone remembering. That is how Class 6 found NAS-01 firmware 900 days old and camera firmware 2,706 days old. A patch policy without a named owner for each asset changes nothing; CT-32 therefore names one per asset and sets the deadlines above.

Lesson 2 — Access control
Least privilege, role-based access, MFA (multi-factor authentication) and the life cycle of accounts

15–24 min · 9 minutes


Key figure of the lesson: Access review: 15 identities, 24 findings, 2 orphan accounts
Who may do what
Access control is the set of rules and mechanisms that decide whether a subject — a person, a program or a device — may perform an action on an object — a file, a share, a mailbox, an administration page. It works in three steps known as AAA (authentication, authorization and accounting). Authentication proves who the subject is. Authorization decides what the subject may do. Accounting records what the subject did, so that every action can be attributed to one person. If one of the three is missing, the other two lose their value: an authorization rule is useless if anyone can authenticate as anyone, and a log is useless if it shows a shared account instead of a person.
Least privilege and related principles
The governing principle is least privilege: every user, and every program acting for a user, receives only the access needed for its tasks, and only while it needs it (NIST [National Institute of Standards and Technology] glossary). Three related principles complete it. Need-to-know applies the same idea to information: you see a file only if your work requires it. Separation of duties prevents one person from completing a sensitive action alone — Kalmia's dual approval of supplier bank changes (CT-16) is an example. Default deny means that what is not explicitly allowed is refused.
Least privilege limits the damage of a stolen account. If a phishing message steals the password of an account that can write only to the Production share, the attacker can encrypt only that share. If the same account is also an administrator of NAS-01, the attacker can delete the snapshots and encrypt every share. The privileges of the account decide the size of the incident.
Access control models
  • Discretionary access control (DAC [discretionary access control]): the owner of an object decides who may use it — the permissions a user sets on a Windows folder that he created.
  • Mandatory access control (MAC [mandatory access control]): the system enforces labels and clearances that users cannot change — the model of classified government systems.
  • Role-based access control (RBAC [role-based access control]): permissions are given to roles (job functions) and people are assigned to roles. A person who changes job changes role instead of accumulating permissions.
  • Attribute-based access control (ABAC [attribute-based access control]): a rule combines attributes of the user, the object and the context — for example, 'finance staff may approve payments only from a company device, during business hours'.
For a 46-person company, RBAC (role-based access control) is the practical choice: twelve roles are easier to review than 31 individual lists of permissions. The RBAC matrix — roles in rows, permissions in columns — becomes the reference against which every real account is compared.
Authentication: multi-factor and legacy protocols
Multi-factor authentication (MFA [multi-factor authentication]) requires two or more different kinds of proof: something you know (a password), something you have (an authenticator app, a hardware security key) or something you are (a fingerprint). With MFA, a stolen password alone no longer opens the account. NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-63B-4 — Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Authenticator Management (July 2025) sets the current rules; phishing-resistant authenticators such as FIDO2 (Fast IDentity Online 2, a standard for phishing-resistant sign-in) security keys and passkeys cannot be replayed through a fake sign-in page, while push approvals can be abused by MFA fatigue (repeated prompts until a tired user approves) unless number matching is used (CISA [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, United States] guidance; ATT&CK [Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge] M1032).
Two weaknesses make MFA (multi-factor authentication) useless. The first is an account that is not enrolled. The second is legacy authentication: older mail protocols such as IMAP (Internet Message Access Protocol) and POP (Post Office Protocol) accept a password alone and therefore bypass MFA. At Kalmia today, MFA protects 2 of 31 Microsoft 365 accounts (KRF-14) and the shared mailbox commandes@ still receives orders over IMAP (KRF-15). Control CT-36 enrols every account and blocks legacy authentication after the order integration is moved.
The life cycle of an account
Accounts follow the joiner-mover-leaver life cycle. A joiner receives an account with the permissions of the role on the first day. A mover who changes job gets the new role and loses the old permissions — removing is as important as adding. A leaver's accounts are disabled on the last working day. An account that stays active after its owner has left is an orphan account: INC-04 in September was one — the account of a former supervisor exported 18,500 loyalty records.
Two other kinds of accounts need special rules. A shared account — one password used by several people — destroys accountability: the logs say 'nas-admin', never a person, and nobody can change the password when one of the four users leaves. The control that catches what the life cycle missed is the access review, also called recertification: at a fixed interval, every permission of every account is compared with the staff list and with the role matrix, and every difference is either justified or removed. Kalmia's CT-14 makes the review quarterly. Tonight's example is the first one.

Key figure of the lesson: Segmentation test: 12 flows, 12 PASS; untrusted devices reaching NAS-01: 9 → 1
Why a flat network is a risk
In Classes 3 and 6 you described Kalmia's head office as a flat network: one subnet, 192.168.10.0/24, without any rule between devices. Every device can open a connection to every other device; in the lab replica, nine devices that nobody trusts — cameras, the printer, the thermostat, the vendor's data logger, phones, and the roaster computer — can open the file-sharing port 445 of NAS-01. A flat network turns one compromised device into a starting point towards all the others. The techniques an attacker uses to go from a first compromised device to other systems inside the network are called lateral movement (MITRE ATT&CK [Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge] M1030 lists segmentation as its mitigation).
Segmentation: zones, VLANs (virtual local area networks) and rules
Network segmentation divides a network into zones — groups of systems with the same sensitivity, function and protection needs — and controls every flow between zones. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security makes it action no. 5 of its Top 10: segment and separate information (ITSM.10.092). Three building blocks are needed:
  1. A VLAN (virtual local area network) (IEEE [Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers] 802.1Q) separates devices logically on the same switches. Each VLAN is its own broadcast domain and receives its own IP (Internet Protocol) subnet.
  2. A firewall (or a router with access control lists) sits between the VLANs (virtual local area networks): no traffic goes from one zone to another without crossing a rule.
  3. A rule set read from top to bottom: the first matching rule decides, and the last rule is default deny — every flow that no rule allows is blocked and logged.
Each allow rule is written as an allow list entry of one business flow: source (zone or host), destination (zone or host), protocol and port, action, and the business reason. The reasons come from the flow inventory you built in Class 3 (flows F-01 to F-16). A rule without a business reason is a finding, because nobody can say whether it may be removed.
East-west traffic and the direction of a flow
Traffic between the internal network and the Internet is called north-south; traffic between internal zones is called east-west. Many small organizations filter north-south traffic at the firewall and leave east-west traffic open, and east-west traffic is exactly what lateral movement uses. Segmentation filters it. One detail matters when you write a rule: a rule allows the side that initiates the connection. NAS-01 records the cameras by opening each camera's video stream (RTSP [Real Time Streaming Protocol], port 554), so the rule is 'NAS-01 → cameras, port 554' and not the reverse — even though the video travels from the cameras to NAS-01.
Forms of segmentation
  • Physical separation (air gap): no network link at all; used for the most critical industrial systems.
  • Logical segmentation: VLANs (virtual local area networks) and firewall rules on shared equipment — tonight's design.
  • Screened subnet (DMZ [demilitarized zone, also called screened subnet]): a zone for servers that the Internet must reach, so that they never sit inside the internal network.
  • Microsegmentation: rules for each workload or application, common in cloud platforms.
  • Zero trust architecture (NIST [National Institute of Standards and Technology] SP [Special Publication] 800-207 — Zero Trust Architecture): no implicit trust based on network location; every access is authenticated and authorized. Segmentation is one of its foundations, not a replacement for it.
Segmentation also serves compliance. For card payments, PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) allows an organization to reduce the number of systems in scope when the payment systems are isolated in their own segment. At Kalmia, the café staff network that mixes the POS (point of sale) tablets with a camera, a menu board and a laptop is the next candidate.
Kalmia's target design
InfoNord proposes six zones for the head office: STAFF (VLAN [virtual local area network] 10, which keeps today's subnet 192.168.10.0/24 so that the office PCs [personal computers] need no change), SERVERS (VLAN 11, NAS-01), MGMT (VLAN 12, the IT [information technology] workstation and the administration interfaces), OT (operational technology) (VLAN 13, ROAST-PC and the data logger), CAM-IOT (VLAN 14, cameras, printer, thermostat) and GUEST (VLAN 15, phones and visitors, Internet only). Seven allow rules carry the business flows; rule R99 denies everything else. Karim Haddad accepts on one condition: nothing that works today may stop working. The design is therefore tested on paper before a single switch port is changed — tonight's example.

Lesson 4 — Security awareness training
A tailored, measured programme: recognize, report, and fix the processes that make deception easy

33–40 min · 7 minutes


Key figure of the lesson: June 2026 test: 46.7 % clicked, 30.0 % entered a password, 10.0 % reported
People are one of the layers
Most of Kalmia's incidents started with a person: INC-02 (a fake change of banking details, stopped by a call-back), INC-04 (a former supervisor's account), INC-05 (phishing text messages sent to baristas), and the phishing test of June 2026 in which 9 of 30 employees entered their password (KRF-29). Social engineering is the manipulation of people so that they perform an action or disclose information that helps the attacker. Its common forms are phishing by e-mail, smishing by text message, vishing by telephone, and business e-mail compromise (BEC [business e-mail compromise]), in which the attacker impersonates a supplier or an executive in order to redirect a payment.
Awareness, training and education
NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-50 Rev. 1 — Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program (September 2024) describes a cybersecurity and privacy learning program with three levels. Awareness focuses attention on security so that people recognize a problem and respond — it is short, frequent and for everyone. Training builds the skills that a role needs: a finance clerk learns the call-back procedure, an administrator learns to separate administrator and everyday accounts. Education builds understanding of the reasons, for the people who design controls. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security makes tailored training action no. 6 of its Top 10 (ITSM.10.093; two-page version for small organizations: ITSAP.10.093), and Control 14 of the CIS (Center for Internet Security) Controls asks for a security awareness and skills training program.
A programme, not a video
  1. Audience analysis: who faces which threat — café staff receive SMS (Short Message Service, text messages) phishing, finance receives fake bank changes, managers are impersonated.
  2. Timing: before first access, then short refreshers. With about 40 % turnover in the cafés (KRF-33), the first module must take place on the first shift, before the POS (point of sale) and Wi-Fi access are given.
  3. Content tied to real cases: Kalmia's own incidents are more convincing than generic examples.
  4. One reporting channel and a no-blame rule: one address or one button, and no sanction for a person who reports a mistake. A report received in five minutes lets the IT (information technology) coordinator block the message for everyone.
  5. Phishing simulations: authorized by management, announced as part of the programme, results reported in aggregate. Under Québec's privacy law, individual results are personal information.
  6. Metrics and improvement: measure, compare with targets, adjust the modules.
Measuring a programme
Four measures describe a phishing simulation. The click rate is the number of people who clicked divided by the number of messages sent. The submission rate is the number who entered credentials divided by the number sent. The report rate is the number who reported the message divided by the number sent. The time to first report is the delay between sending and the first report. For the June 2026 test (30 sent, 14 clicked, 9 submitted, 3 reported) the rates are 46.7 %, 30.0 % and 10.0 %. Kalmia's targets for 2027 are a click rate of 20 % or less, a submission rate of 5 % or less and a report rate of 50 % or more. The report rate is the most useful of the three: it measures whether the employees act as an early warning for the whole company.
Limits of training, and process changes
Training lowers the likelihood that a person is deceived; it never brings it to zero. That is why awareness is one layer among others (Lesson 5): MFA (multi-factor authentication) stops a stolen password, and the call-back procedure stops a fake bank change even when the e-mail convinced someone. Some problems are better solved by changing the process than by training. If Kalmia never sends schedules as links in text messages and uses only the scheduling application, then a text message with a schedule link is suspicious by definition — every barista can apply that rule without technical knowledge.

How much security is enough?
Not every asset needs the same protection. The required level of security of an asset is the strength of protection that it must receive. Resilience
Security tries to prevent an event; resilience accepts that some events will succeed and asks how well the organization continues. NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-160 Vol. 2 Rev. 1 — Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems (December 2021) describes cyber resiliency as the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from and adapt to adverse conditions, stresses, attacks or compromises. The required level of resilience is expressed with the measures of Class 13: the maximum tolerable downtime (MTD [maximum tolerable downtime]), the recovery time objective (RTO [recovery time objective]), the recovery point objective (RPO [recovery point objective]) and the work recovery time (WRT [work recovery time]). For R-01, management set a tolerance of 48 hours for order processing; the Class 13 drill showed an outage of 158 hours today and 16.75 hours with the planned backups, alert and isolation procedure.
Defence in depth
Defence in depth is the design principle of placing several independent layers of controls between a threat and an asset, so that the failure of one layer does not lead to the compromise of the asset. The NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) glossary quotes NIST SP (Special Publication) 800-53 Rev. 5: integrating people, technology and operations capabilities to establish variable barriers across multiple layers and missions of the organization; for industrial systems it quotes the ISA/IEC 62443 wording: the application of multiple countermeasures in a layered or stepwise manner to achieve security objectives. The usual layers are policies and people, physical security, the perimeter, the internal network, the endpoints, identity, applications, data, and detection and response, with recovery behind them.
Three rules make the layers real. Diversity: use different categories of controls (administrative, technical, physical) and different functions (preventive, detective, corrective), so that one technique does not defeat all of them. Independence: no single weakness may defeat two layers at the same time; a password used both for an e-mail account and for the NAS-01 administration is a common cause that defeats the identity layer and the privileged-access layer together. No single point of failure: no one control, device or person whose failure removes the protection.
Why independent layers multiply
If the layers are independent, the probability that an attack passes all of them is the product of the probabilities that each layer lets it through. One layer that stops 70 % of attempts lets 30 % through; three such layers let through 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.027, that is 2.7 %. Real layers are never perfectly independent, which is why the common causes must be searched for and removed. The probabilities of tonight's example are teaching estimates agreed in a workshop, not measurements: their value is to compare designs and to show which layer matters most, not to predict the future.
Kalmia: the path of R-01
The ransomware scenario R-01 must cross seven layers to encrypt NAS-01: the e-mail filter, the employee who receives the message, the identity check of the stolen password, the PC (personal computer) where the malware starts, the network between the PC and NAS-01, the privileged access to NAS-01, and finally detection and response. Today four of these seven layers are almost absent: only 2 of 31 accounts have MFA (multi-factor authentication), the network is flat, the NAS-01 administrator password is shared by four people, and no alert watches for mass file changes. NAS-01 supports Tier 1 processes, so its required profile is HIGH.

Lesson 6 — Assessing the risks introduced by the proposed preventive measures
Every measure is a change: identify, rate and reduce what it introduces before approving it

50–58 min · 8 minutes


Key figure of the lesson: 9 measures, 17 introduced risks: 7 HIGH before safeguards → 1 after
Every measure is a change
A preventive measure changes systems, processes or habits, and every change can fail or produce effects that nobody intended. ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 31000:2018 — Risk management — Guidelines says it in its clause on risk treatment: a treatment, even carefully designed and implemented, may not produce the expected outcome and may have unintended consequences, and the new risks it introduces must themselves be managed. The EZ02 competency asks exactly this of you — criterion 6.2: a relevant assessment of the risks associated with the proposed preventive measures.
Introduced risk and residual risk
An introduced risk (also called secondary risk) is a risk that exists because a treatment was applied. It is different from the residual risk of Class 13, which is what remains of the original risk after the treatment. Example: MFA (multi-factor authentication) on every account lowers R-01 (the residual risk of R-01 is lower) and introduces the risk that a barista without a company phone cannot sign in (a new risk that did not exist before the measure). Both must appear in the plan: the residual risk with its acceptance, the introduced risk with its safeguards.
Where introduced risks come from
  • Availability — an update, a firewall rule or a disabled protocol stops a business process. This is the largest category.
  • Integrity — data are damaged during a migration or an upgrade.
  • Confidentiality — a new tool concentrates secrets (a password vault) and becomes a target; an approval prompt is accepted by a tired user (MFA [multi-factor authentication] fatigue).
  • Usability and productivity — more steps and more helpdesk requests; security fatigue pushes people to work around the control.
  • Compatibility and support — a vendor refuses support on a modified system (ROAST-PC).
  • Privacy — logs and simulation results are personal information (Law 25).
  • Dependency — a new single point of failure (the firewall between all zones) or a dependency on one supplier; and cost beyond the estimate.
A real case shows the size these risks can reach. On July 19, 2024, a content update of a widely used endpoint security product crashed Windows computers around the world; Microsoft estimated that 8.5 million devices were affected (Microsoft, July 20, 2024; vendor analysis: CrowdStrike root cause analysis). A preventive control, deployed everywhere at the same moment, became the cause of an outage. The lessons are those of patch management: staged rollout, testing before broad deployment, and the ability to roll back.
The method: a control impact assessment in six steps
  1. Describe the change: what, on which systems, by whom, when.
  2. Identify what can go wrong, category by category (the list above).
  3. Rate each introduced risk with the same likelihood and impact scales as the register (1 to 5 × 1 to 5), so that it can be compared with the risk the measure removes.
  4. Add safeguards: a test or staging copy, a pilot ring, a maintenance window, a backup and a tested rollback plan, communication to the users, an exception with an end date and a compensating control, monitoring after the change.
  5. Rate again after the safeguards.
  6. Decide: REDESIGN if an introduced risk stays HIGH after the safeguards; APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS if a MEDIUM introduced risk remains (its owner signs an acceptance, as in Class 13) or if the safeguards are mandatory because the risk was HIGH before them; APPROVE if only LOW introduced risks remain; REJECT if the measure removes no risk.
Change management
The safeguards are the content of change management — control CT-39 proposed tonight: a change request, an impact analysis before the change (control CM-4 of NIST [National Institute of Standards and Technology] SP [Special Publication] 800-53 Rev. 5 — Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations), the approval of the owner, a window, a tested rollback, and a verification after the change; NIST SP 800-128 — Guide for Security-Focused Configuration Management of Information Systems describes the same discipline for configurations. The Legal Notice applies it to your own lab work: a defensive change requires the owner's written approval, a window and a rollback (section 7.4). When you propose a measure in Lab 4, you propose its safeguards with it.
