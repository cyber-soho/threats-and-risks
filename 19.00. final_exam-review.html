Part A · Chapter 1

Introduction to Threats and Risks

5 sub-chapters · Topic 1 · Class 1 · about 7 minutes in class

Everything on Saturday's exam rests on this chapter. If the six words of the risk vocabulary are blurry, every later question (the matrix, the register, the controls) becomes a guessing game. Read this one twice.

1.1Key vocabulary — asset, threat, vulnerability, risk, impact, control

The big idea. A risk only exists when three things meet: something of value (an asset), something that can harm it (a threat) and a weakness that lets the harm happen (a vulnerability). The size of the risk depends on how likely the harm is and how bad it would be. A control is anything you put in place to change one of those two numbers.

What you must know

  • An asset is anything that has value for the organization: equipment, software, data, services, people, premises, even reputation. If losing it, changing it or exposing it would hurt, it belongs in the inventory.
  • A threat is a circumstance or an event that has the potential to harm an asset. Separate the threat source (who or what: a criminal group, an employee, a flood, a failing disk) from the threat event (what happens: files are encrypted, records are exported, the power goes out).
  • A vulnerability is a weakness in a system, a configuration, a procedure or a person's habits that a threat can exploit. A missing patch is a vulnerability; so is a shared password; so is "nobody reads the logs".
  • Risk combines the likelihood that a threat exploits a vulnerability with the impact if it does. In this course: score = likelihood × impact, both rated from 1 to 5.
  • Impact is the size of the harm: money lost, hours of interruption, legal obligations, damage to reputation, safety.
  • A control (also called a safeguard or a countermeasure) is a measure that modifies risk. It lowers the likelihood, the impact, or both.
  • Two neighbours of these words: an exploit is the technique or code that uses a vulnerability; an incident is an event that actually harms, or threatens to harm, the confidentiality, integrity or availability of information.

📌 Remember: no asset, nothing to lose; no threat, nothing happens; no vulnerability, no way in.

1.2The CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) triad

The big idea. Every security problem damages at least one of three properties of information. Naming the property tells you what kind of harm you are looking at, and later it tells you which controls make sense.

What you must know

  • Confidentiality: only authorized people, processes or systems can read the information. Lost when data is stolen, leaked, or read by someone who should not see it.
  • Integrity: the information is accurate and complete, and it is changed only by authorized people through authorized means. Lost when data is altered, deleted or forged, even if nobody notices at first.
  • Availability: the information and the systems are there when authorized users need them. Lost when a service stops, files are locked or a site is cut off.
  • One attack can hit more than one property. Modern ransomware copies the data out (confidentiality) and then encrypts it (availability); this pattern is called double extortion.
  • A hash (a fixed-length fingerprint of a file) that no longer matches its recorded value proves that the content changed. It does not, on its own, prove who changed it or why.
  • In the asset inventory, each asset receives a C, I and A rating. Those ratings feed the impact scale later in the course.
Property The question it asks Typical attack Typical control
Confidentiality Who can read this? Theft of the loyalty records Access control, encryption, MFA (multi-factor authentication)
Integrity Can I trust this? A roast profile silently modified Permissions, hashes, change control, logging
Availability Is it there when we need it? Ransomware on NAS-01, DoS (denial of service) on the web shop Backups, redundancy, a tested recovery plan

🧭 At Kalmia: a leak of the 18,500 loyalty members' records is a confidentiality problem; a tampered roast profile on ROAST-PC is an integrity problem that ends up in the coffee itself; a web shop that goes down in December, when a third of online revenue comes in, is an availability problem with a price tag.

⚠️ Exam trap: students often file ransomware under availability only. If the scenario says the attackers threaten to publish the files, confidentiality is also lost.

📌 Remember: C = who reads it, I = can I trust it, A = is it there.

1.3The risk analysis process, step by step

The big idea. A risk analysis is a procedure, not an opinion. The course uses ten steps, and each step produces something you hand in: the context, the inventory (Lab 1), the threat catalogue (Lab 2), the register (Lab 3) and the treatment plan (Lab 4).

What you must know

Step What you do Produced in
1 Prepare and establish the context Purpose, scope, risk criteria (scales, matrix, levels), risk owner Class 1
2 Identify the assets Owner, location, value and a C-I-A (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) rating for each asset Lab 1
3 Identify the threats Threat sources (adversarial and non-adversarial) and threat events Lab 2
4 Identify vulnerabilities and existing controls Scans, configuration reviews, interviews, internal reports Topic 4
5 Determine the likelihood 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain) Lab 3
6 Determine the impact 1 (negligible) to 5 (severe) Lab 3

1.4The risk analysis frameworks of the course

The big idea. You are not inventing risk analysis. Public frameworks give you the vocabulary and the order of operations, and they let two analysts in two companies compare their work.

What you must know

  • The NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) (🔗 https://www.nist.govVT check) CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) 2.0 (🔗 https://www.nist.gov/cyberframeworkVT check), published in February 2024, organizes cybersecurity outcomes into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern is the new one in version 2.0: it covers strategy, roles, policy and risk management decisions.
  • Risk analysis lives mostly in Identify (asset management, risk assessment) and Govern (risk strategy, risk appetite). Your labs map well onto these two functions.
  • NIST SP (Special Publication) 800-30 Revision 1 (🔗 https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/30/r1/finalVT check) is the guide for conducting risk assessments. Its four steps: prepare, conduct, communicate, maintain.
  • ISO/IEC (International Organization for Standardization / International Electrotechnical Commission) 27005 (🔗 https://www.iso.org/standard/80585.htmlVT check) gives guidance on managing information security risks. Its process: context establishment, risk identification, risk analysis, risk evaluation, risk treatment, with communication and consultation, and monitoring and review running alongside. It is the risk companion of ISO/IEC 27001 (🔗 https://www.iso.org/standard/27001VT check) and follows the general model of ISO 31000 (🔗 https://www.iso.org/iso-31000-risk-management.htmlVT check).
  • Risk assessment in ISO/IEC 27005 has three parts: identification, analysis, evaluation. Analysis produces the level; evaluation compares that level with the criteria and decides what needs treatment.
Framework Who publishes it What you use it for in this course
NIST CSF 2.0 NIST (United States) The six functions; placing a control or an activity in the big picture
NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 NIST Threat sources and events, likelihood, impact, the level of risk
ISO/IEC 27005 ISO and IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission) (🔗 https://iec.ch/homepageVT check) The process from context to treatment, risk acceptance
CIS Controls v8.1 Center for Internet Security (🔗 https://www.cisecurity.org/controls/v8-1VT check) Eighteen prioritized practices, starting with inventories

🧭 At Kalmia: InfoNord, the MSP (managed service provider) that runs the firewall FW-HQ, the backups and the helpdesk, asked for a "CSF profile". In practice it means: tell us where Kalmia stands today, function by function, and where it wants to be.

⚠️ Exam trap: NIST CSF 2.0 has six functions, not five. If an option lists Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover and stops there, it is describing version 1.1.

📌 Remember: CSF = what outcomes; SP 800-30 = how to assess; ISO/IEC 27005 = how to manage the whole risk process.

Identifying Information Assets

5 sub-chapters · Topic 2 · Classes 2–3 · Lab 1 · about 7 minutes in class

You cannot protect what you have not listed. Topic 2 turned that sentence into Lab 1: an inventory, a classification, a map of the network and a map of who can touch what. Expect scenario questions where you must spot the missing column or the dangerous permission.

2.1Hardware and software inventories, licences and permissions

The big idea. An inventory is a living table, not a purchase list. Each row says what the asset is, where it is, who answers for it, how valuable it is and who is allowed to administer it. The permissions are part of the inventory because an asset that everyone can administer is an asset nobody controls.

What you must know

  • Hardware inventory fields used in Lab 1: identifier, type, model, location, IP (Internet Protocol) address, MAC (media access control) address, operating system or firmware version, owner, value, C-I-A (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) rating, criticality, status (in service, spare, retired).
  • Reconciliation compares at least two sources (purchase records, a network scan, the helpdesk list). A device seen on the network but missing from the records is an unknown asset; a device in the records but never seen on the network is lost, retired or switched off. Both need an explanation.
  • Software inventory fields: product, version, publisher, where it is installed, licence type, number of licences owned versus installed, support status (supported or end of life), and who may install or update it.
  • Licences come in several forms: perpetual, subscription, per user, per device, and open-source licences with their own conditions. Installing more copies than you own is a compliance problem; running software that no longer receives fixes is a security problem.
  • Associated permissions: who is a local administrator, who manages the device remotely, who can install software. These columns usually reveal the first risk factors of the course.
  • The CIS (Center for Internet Security) Controls v8.1 (🔗 https://www.cisecurity.org/controls/v8-1VT check) start exactly here: Control 1 is the inventory of enterprise assets, Control 2 the inventory of software assets.
Inventory problem Why it matters Typical fix
Unknown device on the office network Nobody patches or monitors it Identify the owner or disconnect it
More installations than licences Legal and financial exposure Remove copies or buy licences
Unsupported version still running No more security fixes Upgrade, replace or isolate
Every user is a local administrator Malware runs with full rights Standard accounts for daily work

🧭 At Kalmia: the hardware reconciliation of Class 2 found devices on the office network that were in no record at all, and the software audit found computers where staff could install anything. Both findings fed the risk factors of Topic 4.

⚠️ Exam trap: the owner of an asset is the person accountable for it (often a manager), not the person who uses it every day and not necessarily the IT (information technology) coordinator.

📌 Remember: an inventory row without an owner is an unfinished row.

2.2Asset classification, ownership and criticality

The big idea. Classification tells people how carefully to handle information; criticality tells management how fast a system must come back after an outage. They are two different questions and the inventory needs both answers.

What you must know

  • Kalmia uses four classification labels, from least to most sensitive: PUBLIC (can be published), INTERNAL (for staff), CONFIDENTIAL (limited to the people who need it, for example HR (human resources) and finance data) and RESTRICTED (the most sensitive: technical details of weaknesses, security configurations).
  • The label follows the most sensitive content. A folder that mixes public price lists with payroll files is classified as the payroll.
  • Owner: accountable for the asset and decides who gets access. Custodian: runs it day to day according to the owner's rules (often IT (information technology) or the MSP (managed service provider)). User: works with it under the rules.
  • A RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix shows who does what for an asset. Only one person is Accountable for each activity.
  • Criticality comes from the business: how long can the organization live without the asset? That tolerance is the MTD (maximum tolerable downtime). The shorter the MTD, the higher the criticality tier.
  • The C-I-A (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) rating of an asset (for example 3-3-2) is a short record of how much each property matters for that asset.
Concept Question Who answers
Classification How sensitive is the information? The owner, using the policy labels
Criticality How long can we live without it? Management, through the business impact analysis
Ownership Who is accountable? Management names the owner
Custody Who operates it day to day? IT or the MSP (managed service provider)

🧭 At Kalmia: Sophie Tremblay is accountable for the HR share on NAS-01; Lucas Martin and InfoNord are custodians. A RACI matrix written in Class 2 made that visible for the first time.

⚠️ Exam trap: "highly confidential" does not automatically mean "highly critical". The loyalty export is sensitive but the café sales can continue without it; the point-of-sale service is not secret at all but the cafés stop without it.

📌 Remember: classification = sensitivity; criticality = tolerable downtime.

2.3Building the inventory with a template and Nmap (Network Mapper)

The big idea. A network scanner tells you what is actually connected and listening, which is often different from what the records say. Nmap (🔗 https://nmap.org/VT check) is the reference tool, and its output becomes inventory rows. Scanning without written authorization is not a lab exercise; it can be an offence.

What you must know

  • Authorization first. Scan only the targets the exercise names, in the lab, with written permission. Section 342.1 of the Criminal Code (🔗 https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-342.1.htmlVT check) covers unauthorized use of a computer; the course rules are in the Legal Notice (🔗 https://cyber-soho.github.io/prevmon/00.01.%20cyber.soho%20-%20legal.htmlVT check).
  • Common options: -sn host discovery only (which hosts are up), -p choose the ports, -sV detect service versions, -O guess the operating system, -oX save the result as XML (Extensible Markup Language) so a script can turn it into rows.
  • Nmap reports six port states: open, closed, filtered, unfiltered, open|filtered and closed|filtered. Open means a service answers; filtered means something (usually a firewall) prevents Nmap from knowing.
  • Common ports to recognize: 22 SSH (Secure Shell), 80 HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol), 443 HTTPS (HTTP Secure), 445 SMB (Server Message Block, Windows file sharing), 3389 RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol), 3306 MySQL, 8443 an alternative HTTPS port often used by administration portals.
  • The scan finds devices and services; the template adds what a scanner cannot know: owner, value, classification, criticality.
192.168.10.20   445/tcp open  microsoft-ds   (SMB file sharing)
192.168.10.1    8443/tcp open https-alt      (administration portal)

🧭 At Kalmia: the authorized scan of the lab replica of 192.168.10.0/24 showed NAS-01 answering on 445 and the firewall's administration portal answering on 8443. The second finding later became a risk factor: that portal also answers from the Internet.

⚠️ Exam trap: a filtered port is not a closed port. It means "I could not tell", so it is an unknown, not a reassurance.

📌 Remember: scan with permission, then describe with the template.

2.4Critical data and services — the business impact analysis

The big idea. Before deciding what to protect first, ask the business which activities hurt most when they stop, and how fast. The BIA (business impact analysis) answers that question with three numbers per process.

What you must know

  • MTD (maximum tolerable downtime): the longest outage the business can survive before the damage becomes unacceptable.
  • RTO (recovery time objective): the target time to bring the process back. The RTO must always be shorter than the MTD, otherwise the plan fails by design.
  • RPO (recovery point objective): the maximum amount of data, measured in time, that the business accepts to lose. A nightly backup means an RPO of up to 24 hours.
  • Each process depends on assets: servers, accounts, suppliers, people. The BIA lists those dependencies so that the protection effort follows the business, not the hardware.
  • Critical services are not always on site: cloud point of sale, Microsoft 365 and a hosted web shop are still Kalmia's services, run by someone else.
Number Plain meaning Rule
MTD How long we can survive the outage Set by management
RTO How long we plan to take to restore RTO < MTD
RPO How much recent data we can lose Decides the backup frequency

🧭 At Kalmia: the Tier 1 processes are café sales, the web shop and wholesale preparation. For the ransomware scenario R-01 the MTD is 48 hours: detection, containment and recovery together must fit inside that window.

⚠️ Exam trap: RPO is about data (how far back you restore), RTO is about time (how long the restore takes). Do not swap them.

📌 Remember: MTD is the wall, RTO is the plan, RPO is the data you can afford to lose.

2.5Network diagrams and the permissions map

The big idea. Two drawings and one table complete Lab 1. The physical diagram shows where things are and how they are cabled; the logical diagram shows how traffic flows (networks, addresses, firewalls). The permissions map shows who can reach which asset, with which rights.

What you must know

  • Physical diagram: rooms, racks, cables, switches, the Internet link, the UPS (uninterruptible power supply). Useful for power cuts, theft and floods.
  • Logical diagram: subnets, VLANs (virtual local area networks), IP (Internet Protocol) ranges, firewall rules, remote access paths, cloud services. Useful for attack paths and segmentation.
  • A flat network puts every device in one subnet. Any infected laptop can then talk directly to the file server, the cameras and the roaster computer.
  • Permissions map: rows are people or groups, columns are assets or folders, cells hold the rights (read, write, administer). It exposes excessive rights, shared accounts and accounts of people who left.
  • Principles to apply when you read it: least privilege (only the rights the task needs), need to know, separation of duties (no single person controls a whole sensitive process), and a periodic access review.
Finding in a permissions map Risk it points to
A shared administrator account No accountability; one stolen password opens everything
A former employee's account still active Insider misuse or account takeover
"Everyone" can write to the finance folder Tampering, ransomware reaches more data
One person approves and pays invoices Fraud without a second check

🧭 At Kalmia: the permissions map showed the shared account adm-lmartin administering NAS-01 and an account of a former employee still able to reach the loyalty console. That second line became R-02 in the register.

⚠️ Exam trap: a logical diagram is not "the same drawing with less detail". It answers a different question: how data moves, not where the boxes sit.

📌 Remember: physical = where; logical = how traffic flows; permissions map = who can do what.

Topic 3 answered two questions: who could attack Kalmia, and how do you know what they are doing this month? The first part is about actors and methods; the second is about reading public sources like an analyst instead of like a worried reader. Lab 2, the threat catalogue, combined the two.

3.1External threat actors and their motivations

The big idea. Threat actors differ by motivation, capability and how they choose their targets. A small coffee roaster is rarely chosen by name; it is usually found because it is reachable and weak. Motivation predicts what the attacker will do once inside.

What you must know

  • Cybercriminals want money: ransomware, fraud, stolen card data, BEC (business email compromise) where a fake supplier asks for a change of bank account. They work as an industry, with groups that rent out ransomware to affiliates.
  • Hacktivists want attention for a cause: DDoS (distributed denial of service) attacks, defaced websites, leaked documents.
  • State-sponsored groups want intelligence, influence or the ability to disrupt; they are patient and well funded, and they sometimes use criminal tools or criminal partners.
  • Opportunistic attackers use ready-made tools against anything that answers on the Internet. Low skill, high volume: they find exposed portals and default passwords within hours.
  • Analysts describe an actor by intent (what it wants), capability (what it can do) and targeting (how it picks victims).
  • The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (🔗 https://www.cyber.gc.ca/enVT check) states in its National Cyber Threat Assessment 2025-2026 (🔗 https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/national-cyber-threat-assessment-2025-2026VT check) that ransomware is the top cybercrime threat facing Canada's critical infrastructure.

Hacktivist Ideology, publicity DDoS on the web shop, defacement
State-sponsored Espionage, disruption Rarely targets a café chain directly; may pass through a supplier

3.2Internal threats — insiders, negligent users, third parties and suppliers

The big idea. Not every threat comes from outside. People who already have access (employees, former employees whose accounts were never closed, suppliers with administrator rights) can cause harm on purpose or by accident, and their actions look legitimate to most controls.

What you must know

  • Malicious insider: a person with legitimate access who misuses it for revenge, money or ideology. Typical tool: data export, sabotage, a logic bomb.
  • Negligent user: no bad intent, but a mistake opens the door: a click on a phishing link, a file sent to the wrong address, a password reused everywhere. Statistically the most frequent internal threat.
  • Third parties and suppliers: managed service providers, software vendors, cleaning crews with badges. Their access is legitimate, so their compromise is your compromise.
  • Former employees whose accounts stay active are a classic gap between HR (human resources) and IT (information technology).
  • NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-30 (🔗 https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/30/r1/finalVT check) also lists non-adversarial sources: accidental errors, structural failures (a disk, a power supply) and environmental events (fire, flood, power cut). They belong in the catalogue too.
  • Typical controls: least privilege, separation of duties, logging and review, an offboarding checklist, supplier contracts with security clauses.

3.3Common attack methods

The big idea. A handful of methods explain most incidents in small organizations. Learn to recognize each one from its symptoms, because exam scenarios describe behaviour, not labels.

What you must know

  • Malware: software inserted without the owner's consent to damage, steal or control (Chapter 4 covers the families). Ransomware encrypts or locks data and demands payment; today it usually steals a copy first.
  • Phishing: a fraudulent message that pushes the reader to click, open, pay or reveal. Variants: spear phishing (targeted at one person or team), whaling (aimed at executives), smishing (by SMS, short message service), vishing (by voice call).
  • Social engineering in general: pretexting (a believable story), baiting (a USB (Universal Serial Bus) stick left in the parking lot), tailgating (following someone through a door).
  • DoS (denial of service) floods or crashes a service; DDoS (distributed denial of service) does it from many machines at once, often a botnet.
  • Credential theft: stealing or guessing login data. Brute force tries many passwords on one account; password spraying tries one common password on many accounts; credential stuffing replays username and password pairs stolen elsewhere; infostealer malware collects saved passwords and session cookies.
What the logs show Most likely method

One failure on each of many accounts, same minute Password spraying

3.4Public information sources — newsletters, advisories, CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) and MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge)

The big idea. A small organization cannot do its own threat research, but it can read the people who do. Four families of public sources cover most needs: newsletters, official advisories, vulnerability records and the catalogue of attacker techniques.

What you must know

  • Cyberthreat newsletters summarize the week's incidents and campaigns. Useful for awareness, but they repeat other sources; trace the claim back to the original.
  • Security advisories: official alerts from the Cyber Centre (🔗 https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/alerts-advisoriesVT check), from CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) (🔗 https://www.cisa.gov/VT check) and from vendors. They name the product, the affected versions, the fixed versions and the recommended action.
  • CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures): the public list of disclosed vulnerabilities, run by the CVE Program (🔗 https://www.cve.org/VT check). Each record has an identifier in the form CVE-year-number (for example CVE-2017-7921), a description and references. The NVD (National Vulnerability Database) (🔗 https://nvd.nist.gov/VT check) adds a CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) score and other details.
  • KEV: CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog (🔗 https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalogVT check) lists vulnerabilities that are confirmed as exploited in real attacks. A KEV entry moves a vulnerability to the top of the patch list.
  • MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge) (🔗 https://attack.mitre.org/VT check) describes attacker behaviour. Tactics are the goals (TA0001 Initial Access, TA0003 Persistence, TA0040 Impact); techniques are the ways to reach them (T1566 Phishing); sub-techniques are more precise (T1566.001 spear-phishing attachment).

3.5Reading and rating a threat report, and building the threat catalogue

The big idea. An analyst reads a report with a fixed set of questions, rates the source and the information separately, decides whether it applies to the organization, and records the decision with its reasons. The threat catalogue is the table where those decisions live.

What you must know

  • The seven questions: (1) publisher, date and marking; (2) subject; (3) evidence: what is observed fact and what is assessment; (4) applicability to our assets, with a relevance level; (5) behaviour, as ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge) identifiers and indicators of compromise; (6) recommendations and who at our company can act; (7) decision and where it is recorded.
  • Two independent scales (the Admiralty or NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organization) system): source reliability from A (completely reliable) to E (unreliable), F when it cannot be judged; information credibility from 1 (confirmed by other independent sources) to 5 (improbable), 6 when it cannot be judged. An official alert repeating a vendor advisory is A1; an anonymous post with no product and no version is F6.
  • TLP (Traffic Light Protocol) 2.0 (🔗 https://www.first.org/tlp/VT check) says who may receive a report: CLEAR (public), GREEN (community), AMBER (the organization and its clients who need it), AMBER+STRICT (the organization only), RED (named recipients only).
  • Estimative language: words such as "likely" or "almost certainly" correspond to probability ranges; confidence (low, moderate, high) describes the quality of the evidence, not the probability.
  • Attribution to a named group is an assessment: report who made it ("CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) assesses that…"), never repeat it as a fact.
  • Relevance: High when an exposed asset is matched directly, Moderate when the match is indirect or goes through a supplier, Low or none otherwise.
  • Decisions: act now, add to the catalogue, watch, or discard, always with the ratings and reasons on the same line.

Malware in Depth: Families, Vectors and Persistence

5 sub-chapters · Module Inside the Machine · Lessons 1–2 · about 7 minutes in class

This chapter comes from the attached module Inside the Machine: Operating System Security & Malware (Lessons 1 and 2). Its worked example uses another fictional company, BlueFern Trading Inc.; here the same ideas are applied to Kalmia. Malware is the tool behind most of the risks in your register, so expect several exam questions where you classify a behaviour or match a control to a stage.

4.1What malware is and the three classification questions

The big idea. Malware is defined by intent and consent, not by size or programming language. You classify a sample by what it does, using three questions in a fixed order, and the answer tells you what to do first.

What you must know

  • NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-83 (🔗 https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/83/r1/finalVT check), the guide to malware incident prevention and handling, describes malware as a program or part of a program inserted into a system without the owner's consent, to damage data, run intrusive or destructive code, or otherwise compromise the confidentiality, integrity or availability of the system.
  • Tool versus technique: phishing or password guessing is a human technique that delivers the tool; the malware is the tool that does the damage. Keep them in separate columns.
  • The three questions, in order:
    1. Self-replication: does it copy itself to other files or computers without a person doing the copying?
    2. Host dependency: does it need a host (another program, a document) or a user action to run, or is it stand-alone?
    3. Goal of the payload: does it destroy, encrypt, steal, control, hide, advertise, or wait for a condition?
  • The payload is the part of the code that performs the intended damage, as opposed to the parts that spread it or hide it.
Question Yes leads to… No leads to…
1 Copies itself? Question 2 (virus or worm) Question 3 (the goal decides)
2 Needs a host and a user? Virus Worm
3 Goal of the payload Encrypt → ransomware · receive orders → bot · hide → rootkit · collect → spyware · ads → adware · wait for a condition → logic bomb · disguise and open a backdoor → trojan —

4.3Infection vectors

The big idea. An infection vector is the path by which malware reaches a computer and runs for the first time. It usually combines a technical channel with a human decision. Five vectors explain almost every infection in a small company.

What you must know

  • E-mail attachments and macros: a document carries VBA (Visual Basic for Applications) code that runs when the user clicks Enable content. The macro is usually a dropper (or loader): a small first stage whose only job is to download and start the real malware. Since 2022 Microsoft blocks macros in files marked with the MOTW (Mark of the Web), a flag added to anything that came from the Internet (🔗 https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/deployoffice/security/internet-macros-blockedVT check); attackers moved to containers that may not carry the flag (archives, disc images, shortcut files).
  • Drive-by downloads: a web page exploits a browser vulnerability or shows a fake "your browser is out of date" prompt. Advertising networks can carry the bait (malvertising), so an unpatched browser is a risk even on normal sites.
  • Removable media: a USB (Universal Serial Bus) stick with a file that a curious user opens, or a device that pretends to be a keyboard and types commands. Windows has disabled automatic execution from USB since Windows 7, so a human step is now needed, and it still happens.
  • Supply chain: the malware arrives inside something trusted, such as a vendor update or a software library. NotPetya (2017) travelled in the update of an accounting program; in 2020 a network-management vendor's signed update carried a backdoor to thousands of customers. The victim did nothing wrong at the moment of infection.
  • Exposed services and weak credentials: RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol), SSH (Secure Shell), FTP (File Transfer Protocol) or a database answering on the Internet, combined with a known flaw or a guessable password.
  • Why the invoice works: Windows hides known file extensions by default, so Invoice_0923.pdf.exe appears as Invoice_0923.pdf with a document icon, sent to a busy finance employee. The weakness is the default configuration and the missing second check, not the person.

4.4Persistence and obfuscation

The big idea. Once running, malware wants two things: to survive a restart (persistence) and to avoid being noticed (obfuscation and defence evasion). Each technique tells the defender where to look and which control to add.

What you must know

  • Persistence is its own tactic in MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques and Common Knowledge), TA0003 (🔗 https://attack.mitre.org/tactics/TA0003/VT check).
  • Windows persistence locations: registry Run and RunOnce keys, the Startup folder, scheduled tasks, new services, WMI (Windows Management Instrumentation) event subscriptions. The Sysinternals tool Autoruns lists every autostart location on one screen (🔗 https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sysinternals/downloads/autorunsVT check).
  • Linux persistence locations: cron jobs, systemd units and timers, shell start-up files (.bashrc, .profile), a new account or an added SSH (Secure Shell) key in authorized_keys.
  • Obfuscation keeps the behaviour but hides the appearance. Packing compresses or encrypts the executable and unpacks it in memory, so the file on disk matches no signature. Polymorphism produces a different form at every copy.
  • Living off the land: using programs already on the machine and signed by the vendor (PowerShell, certutil, mshta, rundll32 on Windows; curl, bash, python on Linux), so there is no new executable to detect.
  • Impairing defences: switching off the antivirus, clearing event logs, changing timestamps. Tamper protection and logs forwarded to another machine are the counter-measures.

Identifying Risk Factors and Vulnerabilities

5 sub-chapters · Topic 4 · Classes 6 and 9 · module Lesson 3 · about 7 minutes in class

Topic 4 moved from "who could attack" to "where are we weak". You looked at the network design, at old and unpatched software, at a scanner's report, at the security settings of the operating system (module Lesson 3) and at Kalmia's own internal documents. A risk factor is any condition that raises the likelihood or the impact of a risk; most of them are vulnerabilities, some are missing controls.

5.1Network architecture — segmentation, exposed services, remote access, wireless

The big idea. The way a network is built decides how far an attacker can go after the first foothold. A flat network, services exposed to the Internet, remote access without a second factor and a shared Wi-Fi are risk factors even when every computer is patched.

What you must know

  • Segmentation splits the network into zones (servers, office computers, cameras, guests, payment terminals) with rules between them, usually VLANs (virtual local area networks) plus firewall rules. Without it, one infected laptop can reach everything.
  • Exposed services: every service that answers from the Internet is attack surface. Administration portals, RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol), SSH (Secure Shell) and databases should not be reachable by the whole world.
  • Remote access: a VPN (virtual private network) without MFA (multi-factor authentication), remote-control tools installed by staff, or vendor access that is always on, all widen the entry points.
  • Wireless: guest Wi-Fi must be separated from the office network; a shared Wi-Fi password that never changes, or old encryption, is a risk factor. In cafés, the customer network must never share a segment with the point-of-sale devices.
  • An attack path usually chains several factors: an exposed portal plus a weak password plus a flat network leads straight to the file server.

File server accepts SMB (Server Message Block) from any device Ransomware on any device reaches the shares Allow SMB only from the office computers that need it

5.2Vulnerable and obsolete software — end of life, missing patches, default configurations

The big idea. Three software conditions keep appearing in incident reports: software that no longer receives fixes, fixes that were published but never installed, and factory settings that were never changed.

What you must know

  • EOL (end of life): the vendor no longer publishes security fixes. Windows 10 support ended on October 14, 2025 (🔗 https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/end-of-supportVT check), except for machines enrolled in ESU (Extended Security Updates). Old database versions and camera firmware reach end of life too.
  • Missing patches: WannaCry hit, in May 2017, computers that lacked a patch published two months earlier. Patching needs an owner, a schedule and a check.
  • Default configurations: factory passwords, unnecessary services left on, sample accounts, administration open to all. The Mirai botnet was built from devices that still used their default passwords.
  • An EOL product cannot be "patched later"; the only options are to replace it, upgrade it, isolate it, or accept the risk in writing for a limited time.
  • The CIS (Center for Internet Security) Benchmarks (🔗 https://www.cisecurity.org/cis-benchmarksVT check) give the secure settings that replace the defaults.
Condition Example What the analyst writes
End of life Windows 10 without ESU No fixes will come: replace, isolate or accept with a date
Missing patch Browser two versions behind Patch owner and deadline

5.3Reading a vulnerability scan report

The big idea. A scanner lists potential weaknesses automatically. Your job is to read the report critically: what was scanned, how, which findings are real, and which ones matter most for this organization.

What you must know

  • Read the header first: scope (which addresses), date, scanner, and whether the scan was authenticated (logged in, sees installed software) or unauthenticated (sees only what answers on the network).
  • Each finding gives the host, the port, the CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) identifier when one exists, a CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) (🔗 https://www.first.org/cvss/VT check) score, the evidence and a solution.
  • CVSS severity bands (versions 3.1 and 4.0): None 0.0, Low 0.1–3.9, Medium 4.0–6.9, High 7.0–8.9, Critical 9.0–10.0.
  • CVSS measures technical severity, not your risk. 5.4Operating-system security settings as risk factors

The big idea. The operating system decides what every program may do; the component that allows or denies each request is the reference monitor. Five settings decide whether malware gets more authority than it should: accounts and privileges, file permissions, services, patching and logging (module Lesson 3).

What you must know

  • Accounts and privileges. Windows: members of the local Administrators group are all-powerful; UAC (User Account Control) keeps even administrators at standard rights until an elevation is confirmed; the built-in Administrator has a SID (security identifier) ending in -500; the Guest account should be disabled. Linux: root has UID (user identifier) 0; regular users start at 1000; sudo grants temporary root rights under the rules of /etc/sudoers and logs each use. Least privilege: daily work as a standard user.
  • File permissions. Linux shows read, write, execute for owner, group and others; octal values add read 4, write 2, execute 1 per set. rwxr-x--- = 750; drwxrwxrwx = 777, world-writable, dangerous. An s in the owner's execute position (-rwsr-xr-x, 4755) is the setuid bit: the program runs with its owner's rights, often root. Windows uses NTFS (New Technology File System) ACLs (access control lists); the equivalent of 777 is Everyone: Full control. Share and NTFS permissions are combined and the more restrictive wins.
  • Services. Each background service (a daemon on Linux) is attack surface. Inventory them (services.msc, Get-Service; systemctl list-units --type=service, ss -tlnp), disable what is not needed, restrict the rest.
  • Patching. Microsoft publishes on the second Tuesday of each month (Patch Tuesday) through its Security Update Guide (🔗 https://msrc.microsoft.com/update-guideVT check); Linux uses the package manager (apt update && apt upgrade) and can patch automatically with unattended-upgrades (🔗 https://ubuntu.com/server/docs/how-to/software/automatic-updates/VT check).
  • Logging. Windows Security log: 4624 successful logon, 4625 failed logon (🔗 https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-10/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4625VT check), 4720 account created, 4732 member added to a local group, 4698 scheduled task created, 1102 audit log cleared; System log 7045 new service. Linux: /var/log/auth.log lines such as Failed password for invalid user admin from 198.51.100.7. Logs help only if they are kept, protected and read, ideally on a central collector.

5.5Risk factors by resource type and internal information sources

The big idea. The competency asks you to find risk factors in four kinds of resources (material, software, organizational and human) and to extract them from the organization's own documents. What you must know

  • Material factors: devices, premises, power, media. Example: a single disk in NAS-01, a closet UPS (uninterruptible power supply) with an old battery.
  • Software factors: programs, firmware, configurations, cloud services. Example: unsupported firmware, default settings.
  • Organizational factors: processes, documents, contracts, roles. Example: no offboarding procedure, no backup test, a supplier contract without security clauses.
  • Human factors: knowledge, behaviour, capacity. Example: no awareness training, one half-time IT (information technology) coordinator for 46 people.

6.1Likelihood and impact scales; qualitative and quantitative assessment

The big idea. Two analysts can only compare their ratings if they use the same written scales. A qualitative assessment rates with defined categories; a quantitative one puts dollars and frequencies on the risk. Most small organizations start qualitative and add numbers for their top risks.

What you must know

  • Likelihood is the chance that the threat event happens and succeeds within a period; impact is the size of the consequence if it does. Kalmia rates both from 1 to 5 (scales in Chapter 1, section 1.3).
  • Qualitative assessment: words or ordinal numbers, combined in a matrix. Fast, works without financial data, easy to explain, and it can include harms that are hard to price (safety, reputation). Limits: it depends on judgment and its bands hide differences. The protection against subjectivity is documentation: every level cites its evidence.
  • Ratings are ordinal: they give an order, not a distance. A score of 16 is not "twice as risky" as 8.
  • Quantitative assessment uses money and frequency:
    • AV (asset value): what the asset is worth, including the cost of interruption.
    • EF (exposure factor): the fraction of that value lost in one event (0 to 1).
    • SLE (single loss expectancy) = AV × EF.
    • ARO (annualized rate of occurrence): expected events per year (0.25 = once every four years).
    • ALE (annualized loss expectancy) = SLE × ARO.
  • Four moments of a risk: inherent (as if no control existed), current (with today's controls, what Lab 3 rates), residual (after the planned treatment), target (the level management wants by a date).

6.2Effects on CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability), complexity of exploitation and spread to other systems

The big idea. Three refinements make a rating realistic: rate the impact on each security property and keep the worst, ask how hard the exploitation really is, and ask how far it could spread once it starts.

What you must know

  • Impact per property: rate confidentiality, integrity and availability separately for the scenario, then keep the highest as the impact level. A ransomware scenario with data theft can be 4 for C, 3 for I and 5 for A: impact 5. This is the high-water mark principle of FIPS (Federal Information Processing Standards) 199 (🔗 https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/199/finalVT check), which rates each objective Low, Moderate or High and gives the system the highest of the three.
  • Complexity of exploitation mostly moves the likelihood. Look at: is there a public exploit or a KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) entry, does it need prior access or privileges, does it need a user to click, does it need special conditions or skills? Easy, public, remote and unauthenticated means high likelihood.
  • Spread (lateral movement) mostly moves the impact. It depends on segmentation, shared or reused credentials, trust between systems (a backup server that can write everywhere), and how many systems share the same weakness.
  • The blast radius of a scenario is the set of systems that could be affected if it succeeds. A flat network and a shared administrator password give a large blast radius.
  • Spread also changes the response: a worm or a human operator moving between machines turns a one-laptop incident into a company-wide one within hours.

6.3The organization's ability to respond to an incident

The big idea. Two companies with the same weakness do not carry the same risk if one can detect, contain and recover in hours while the other needs weeks. Response capability shortens the outage, so it changes the impact, and good detection can stop an attack before its last stage.

What you must know

  • Elements of response capability: a written response plan with named roles, an up-to-date contact list (including the MSP (managed service provider), the insurer, the police and the privacy regulator), detection (logs that someone reads, alerts on suspicious behaviour), backups that were actually restored in a test, exercises, and supplier support written into contracts.
  • Kalmia's readiness index scores eight criteria from 0 to 3; add the eight scores and divide by 24 to get a percentage of readiness. A low index argues for a higher impact rating, because the outage will last longer.
  • Compare the expected outage (time to detect + time to contain + time to recover) with the MTD (maximum tolerable downtime) of the process. If the sum exceeds the MTD, the impact is at the top of the scale.
  • NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-61 Revision 3 (🔗 https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/finalVT check) places incident response inside overall cybersecurity risk management and organizes it around the CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) 2.0 functions; the six-step malware playbook of Chapter 8 is its practical form for a small company.
  • In the module's example, a 14-hour gap between infection and spread was visible in the logs and nobody looked. Detection capability is part of the risk rating, not an afterthought.

7.1Risk treatment options — reduce, transfer, avoid, accept

The big idea. There are only four things you can do with a risk. Each option changes something different, and the exam loves scenarios where you must name the option from a description.

What you must know

  • Reduce (also called mitigate or modify): add or improve controls that lower the likelihood, the impact, or both. The most common option.
  • Transfer (also called share): move part of the consequences to someone else, typically through cyber insurance or a contract. The risk still exists, and the organization stays accountable to its customers and regulators.
  • Avoid: stop the activity that creates the risk, or never start it. Example: stop storing a type of data you do not need.
  • Accept (also called retain): keep the risk knowingly, with a written decision by the person who has the authority. Accepting is a decision, not neglect.
  • Options can be combined: reduce R-01 with backups and segmentation, and transfer part of the remaining financial impact to an insurer.
  • Kalmia's treatment policy has six rules (TP-1 to TP-6) that tell the analyst which option fits which situation. One of them is easy to remember: no HIGH or CRITICAL risk is only accepted.

7.2Types of controls and matching controls to risks

The big idea. Every control can be described with two separate questions: what is it made of (its category) and when does it act (its function)? Answering both prevents plans that are all technology and no procedure, or all prevention and no detection.

What you must know

  • Categories: administrative (policies, procedures, training, contracts, background checks), technical (also called logical: MFA (multi-factor authentication), firewalls, encryption, antivirus, backups software), physical (locks, badges, cameras, UPS (uninterruptible power supply), fire suppression).
  • Functions: preventive (stops the event before it happens), detective (reveals it while or after it happens), corrective (limits the damage and restores normal operation).
  • Some courses add deterrent, compensating or recovery functions; this course uses the three above.
  • Matching: for each risk factor in the register, name the control that addresses it. A traceability matrix links risk factor → control → risk, and shows the gaps (risk factors without any control) and the controls that address nothing important.
  • One control can serve several risks: segmentation reduces ransomware spread and protects the cameras.

7.3Applicability by risk level and cost versus benefit

The big idea. A control is applicable when it fits the level of the risk and when its yearly cost makes sense compared with the yearly loss it prevents. Two simple rules and two formulas carry this section.

What you must know

  • Applicability rule at Kalmia: a CRITICAL risk needs controls covering three functions (preventive, detective and corrective), a HIGH risk two, a MEDIUM risk one.
  • Annualized cost of a control = one-time cost ÷ useful life in years + yearly cost.
  • Net benefit = (ALE before − ALE after) − annualized cost. ALE (annualized loss expectancy) comes from Chapter 6.
  • ROSI (return on security investment) = net benefit ÷ annualized cost. A positive ROSI means the control saves more than it costs.

  • Compare the total plan with the budget. When it does not fit, change the schedule, move a line to another budget, or ask management to accept the remaining risk in writing.

7.4Continuity — backups, redundancy, BCP and DRP

The big idea. Some measures exist so that the business keeps running when prevention fails. Backups protect data, redundancy protects service, the BCP (business continuity plan) keeps critical processes going with workarounds, and the DRP (disaster recovery plan) brings the IT (information technology) back.

What you must know

  • 3-2-1 rule: three copies of the data, on two different types of media, one copy off-site. Because ransomware hunts for backups, that off-site copy must also be offline or immutable (it cannot be modified or deleted for a set period).
  • 3-2-1-1-0: the extended version adds one offline or immutable copy and zero errors when restores are verified.
  • A backup that has never been restored is only a hypothesis. Test restores give you the real RTO (recovery time objective) and confirm the RPO (recovery point objective).
  • Redundancy removes single points of failure: two disks in a mirror, a second Internet link, a UPS (uninterruptible power supply). Redundancy is not a backup: a mirror copies the encryption or the deletion instantly.
  • BCP: how the business continues during the disruption (manual sales, a paper order form, a backup supplier). DRP: how IT restores systems (who declares the disaster, the order of recovery, the contacts, the procedures).
  • The order of recovery follows the business impact analysis: Tier 1 processes first.

What you must know

  • Residual risk = the risk that remains after the planned controls are in place. It is rated with the same scales and matrix as the current risk.
  • Risk appetite: the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or keep. The band limits and required actions of the matrix express Kalmia's appetite.

  • Accepting a risk never means forgetting it: it stays in the register, with its owner and its review date.

8.1The standard response to malware — isolate, identify, contain, eradicate, recover, learn

The big idea. A standard response (a playbook) is a numbered sequence of actions with a named role for each one, written before the incident. At 08:10 on a Monday, nobody should have to invent the order. The six steps below map onto the incident-response life cycle of NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) (🔗 https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/finalVT check): preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, post-incident activity.

What you must know

Step Action Do Do not
1 Isolate Disconnect the machine from the network (cable, Wi-Fi, switch port) Keep it powered on; call IT (information technology) Power it off; keep working on it
2 Identify Establish the family, the hosts, the time and the vector Read the antivirus or EDR (endpoint detection and response) console, the ransom note, file names, Event IDs 4625, 4720, 7045, the Linux auth log, the mail gateway; write times down Guess; run tools that modify files
3 Contain Limit the damage without destroying evidence Disable compromised accounts; change the shared admin password from a clean machine; block the C2 (command-and-control) address; take shares offline; copy the note, the sample and the logs to labelled media Reconnect the backup drive; pay; post online
4 Eradicate Remove the malware and the way it came in Remove persistence; for rootkits and ransomware wipe and reinstall from known-good media; close the vector Trust a "cleaned" compromised system
5 Recover Restore data and services in business order Use a clean, offline, test-restored backup; Tier 1 first; monitor for weeks Restore from a copy that was attached during the encryption
6 Learn Incident report and changes Timeline, root cause, cost, what worked, the changes; notify the parties the law and contracts require Skip the report; blame the user
  • Why keep it powered on? Volatile memory holds evidence (running processes, network connections, sometimes encryption keys), and some families react to a shutdown.
  • IOCs (indicators of compromise): observable signs such as file hashes, the C2 address or the sender's address. You search the other computers for them.
  • Roles: the discoverer disconnects and calls; the IT lead runs steps 1 to 5 and keeps the timeline; the CEO (chief executive officer) decides on money, customers and the press and is the only voice to journalists; the CFO (chief financial officer) talks to the payment provider and the insurer; outside help includes an incident-response firm and the police.
  • Nobody, in the first hour: pays the ransom (no guarantee of a working key, it funds crime, it may breach sanctions rules, and the stolen copy stays with the criminals), powers machines off, reconnects the backup drive, wipes before evidence is saved, or posts about the incident.
  • Spyware variant: assume every password typed on that machine is known; change them all from a different, clean device before anything else.
  • Who is informed in Canada: under Québec's Law 25 (🔗 https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/P-39.1VT check), a confidentiality incident that presents a risk of serious injury is reported to the CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec) (🔗 https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca/VT check) and to the affected persons, and recorded in a register; under PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act) (🔗 https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/VT check), a breach with a real risk of significant harm is reported to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada. Ransomware is reported to the local police, the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (🔗 https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/VT check) and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (🔗 https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/have-you-been-victim-cybercrimeVT check). The Cyber Centre's ransomware playbook (🔗 https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/ransomware-playbook-itsm00099VT check) is the Canadian reference; No More Ransom (🔗 https://www.nomoreransom.org/VT check) offers free decryptors for some families.

🧭 At Kalmia: if ransom notes appear on NAS-01, Lucas Martin unplugs NAS-01 and the affected laptops from the switch, leaves them on, and calls InfoNord. Nadia Roy speaks to the press; Sophie Tremblay calls the insurer. Nobody touches the USB (Universal Serial Bus) backup disk "to see if it still works".

⚠️ Exam trap: "power off the server to stop the encryption" sounds responsible and is wrong. Disconnecting stops the spread just as well and keeps the evidence.

📌 Remember: isolate, identify, contain, eradicate, recover, learn: and never power off first.

8.2Preventive measures — hardening, patch management, access control, segmentation, awareness

The big idea. Prevention removes the defaults and gaps that the attack chain relies on. Each preventive measure should be traceable to a risk factor in the register and to an arrow of the ransomware timeline.

What you must know

  • Hardening: configure a system to expose the smallest possible attack surface (remove or disable what is not needed, restrict the rest) and record the result as a baseline configuration that every machine of that type must match. The usual source is the CIS (Center for Internet Security) Benchmarks (🔗 https://www.cisecurity.org/cis-benchmarksVT check).
  • Patch management: an inventory of what runs, priorities (KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) entries and critical, Internet-facing systems first), a test, a deployment, a verification, and a named owner with a date. End-of-life software is replaced or isolated.
  • Access control: least privilege, named accounts instead of shared ones, a unique local administrator password per machine, MFA (multi-factor authentication), rights by role, and an offboarding checklist that disables accounts the same day.
  • Network segmentation: separate zones for servers, office computers, cameras, guests and payment devices; administration only through the VPN (virtual private network).
  • Security awareness training: short, regular sessions and phishing simulations, an easy way to report a suspicious message, and no blame for reporting.
  • Other baseline items from the module's 15-item checklist: block macros from the Internet and show file extensions, application allowlisting, disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows (🔗 https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/operating-system-security/data-protection/bitlocker/VT check), LUKS (Linux Unified Key Setup) on Linux), USB (Universal Serial Bus) storage restricted, screen lock after ten minutes, security logging forwarded to a collector, offline tested backups, antivirus and EDR (endpoint detection and response) with tamper protection.
Checklist area Example item Why
Accounts Daily work as a standard user Malware runs with the rights of the user who opened it
Patches Automatic updates, monthly check with an owner WannaCry hit machines missing a two-month-old patch
Services SSH (Secure Shell) and RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol) only through the VPN Every listening service is attack surface
Permissions No Everyone: Full control, no world-writable folder Limits what ransomware can reach
Logging Forward logs; review 4625 / 4720 / 7045 weekly The infection was visible in the logs
Backup 3-2-1, offline or immutable, quarterly restore test An attached drive is encrypted with the server
Endpoint Disk encryption on every laptop A lost laptop is not a data breach

🧭 At Kalmia: the first week of the plan (from November 23) brings MFA on all 31 Microsoft 365 accounts (CT-36) and named NAS-01 administrator accounts replacing the shared adm-lmartin (CT-10); the offline backup (CT-07) follows over three weeks.

⚠️ Exam trap: hardening lowers the probability of an infection; an offline backup removes the attacker's leverage. They solve different parts of the problem, so a good plan needs both.

📌 Remember: remove the defaults, patch on a date, least privilege, segment, train.

8.3Endpoint protection — antivirus, EDR (endpoint detection and response) and application allowlisting

The big idea. Endpoint tools detect or block malicious programs on each computer. Signatures catch known samples; behaviour catches what signatures miss; allowlisting refuses anything that was not approved in the first place.

What you must know

  • Signature-based detection compares files with a database of known patterns and hashes: fast and precise, but blind to packed, polymorphic or brand-new samples.
  • Heuristic or behavioural detection watches what a program does (for example opening and rewriting hundreds of documents in a minute) and stops it whatever its name; the price is some false positives.
  • Sandboxing runs a suspicious attachment in an isolated environment before it reaches the user.
  • EDR (endpoint detection and response) goes further than antivirus: an agent records process starts, file changes, registry writes and network connections, correlates them into detections ("Word started PowerShell, which downloaded an executable") and lets IT (information technology) isolate a computer remotely with one click. That is step 1 of the playbook done from a desk.
  • Application allowlisting reverses the logic: only explicitly approved programs run (by publisher signature, path or hash). Windows offers AppLocker and App Control for Business (🔗 https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/application-security/application-control/app-control-for-business/VT check); on Linux, similar effects come from mounting user-writable areas with noexec and from AppArmor or SELinux.
  • The common mistake is rarely the choice of product. It is leaving the protection disabled, outdated, or excluded from the folders that matter.
Method Catches Misses
Signature Known samples New, packed, polymorphic samples
Behavioural Mass encryption, suspicious chains Quiet, slow activity; may raise false alarms
Allowlisting Any unapproved executable Abuse of approved tools (living off the land)
EDR Correlated chains; allows remote isolation Nothing works if nobody watches the console

🧭 At Kalmia: an executable disguised as an invoice would not start on a laptop with allowlisting, whatever the antivirus says about it. That is why allowlisting breaks the ransomware timeline at its download stage.

⚠️ Exam trap: EDR does not replace backups. It shortens detection and allows isolation; recovery still needs a clean copy.

📌 Remember: signatures know the past, behaviour watches the present, allowlisting decides in advance.

8.4Required level of security and resilience, defence in depth and risks introduced by measures

The big idea. The required level of security comes from the risk register and the business, not from the longest product catalogue. Layers of independent controls (defence in depth) make sure one failure does not open everything, and every new control must be checked for the risks it brings.

What you must know

  • Required level of security: set by the risk level, the appetite, legal and contractual obligations, and the business impact analysis. A CRITICAL risk demands more layers than a LOW one.
  • Resilience: the ability to prepare for, withstand, recover from and adapt to an incident. It mixes prevention, detection, response and continuity.
  • Defence in depth: independent layers (policies and people, physical security, network perimeter, internal segmentation, host hardening, application controls, data protection, monitoring and recovery). An attacker who passes one layer meets the next.
  • Risks introduced by preventive measures must be identified and rated:
    • MFA (multi-factor authentication) can lock out staff when a phone is lost; push notifications can be abused by "MFA fatigue" spam.
    • A password vault concentrates every secret in one place.
    • A patch can break an old application, for example the software that controls the roaster.
    • A segmentation error can cut the cafés off from the point-of-sale service.
    • An EDR (endpoint detection and response) agent runs with high privileges and depends on its vendor.
  • Each introduced risk receives a rating, an owner and, if kept, a written acceptance, just like the original risks.
Measure Risk it introduces How to limit it
MFA on all accounts Lock-outs; push fatigue Recovery codes; number matching
Password vault Single point of compromise Strong master credential with MFA; vault access logging
Automatic patching A patch breaks ROAST-PC Test first on a spare machine; maintenance window
Segmentation Business traffic blocked Map flows first; change window; rollback plan

🧭 At Kalmia: IR-15, the risk introduced by the password vault, is rated MEDIUM and carries a written acceptance; the patch policy keeps ROAST-PC out of automatic updates and tests its updates first.

⚠️ Exam trap: "more controls always means less risk" is false. A badly planned control can create a new HIGH risk; that is why introduced risks are assessed.

📌 Remember: layers that fail independently, and a risk check for every new control.

8.5Communicating the analysis, professional ethics and priority management

The big idea. An analysis that nobody understands, or that leaks to the wrong people, has failed. The report is written for each audience at the right level of detail and classification, the analyst behaves ethically with what he learns, and the plan says clearly what gets fixed first and why.

What you must know

  • Report structure used at Kalmia: S1 executive summary, S2 risk register summary, S3 treatment and prevention plan, S4 budget and open decisions, S5 technical annex, S6 notice to all employees, S7 public statement.
  • Audience: management reads business language (money, interruption, decisions needed); the MSP (managed service provider) needs the technical annex; staff need short instructions; the public statement says only what is safe to publish.
  • Classification and marking: each recipient receives only the sections needed, with a label (PUBLIC, INTERNAL, CONFIDENTIAL, RESTRICTED) and, when shared outside, a TLP (Traffic Light Protocol) (🔗 https://www.first.org/tlp/VT check) marking such as TLP:AMBER+STRICT for the technical annex sent to InfoNord.
  • Ethics and confidentiality: work only within your authorization; keep findings confidential without time limit (Legal Notice (🔗 https://cyber-soho.github.io/prevmon/00.01.%20cyber.soho%20-%20legal.htmlVT check)); report honestly, without exaggerating or hiding results; declare conflicts of interest; remove personal information that is not needed (data minimization) before sharing evidence.
  • Priority management: rank by risk level and the deadlines of the bands (CRITICAL within 7 days, HIGH within 30 days), handle the essential controls first (Kalmia follows the Cyber Centre's (🔗 https://www.cyber.gc.ca/enVT check) baseline advice: an incident response plan, patching, strong authentication and backups), respect dependencies (the camera fix waits for the camera VLAN (virtual local area network)), and avoid frozen periods (no risky change to the web shop during the November–December peak).

⚠️ Exam trap: the executive summary is not a short copy of the technical annex. It states the risks, the decisions needed and the budget in business terms, without configuration details.

📌 Remember: right content, right reader, right label; fix the critical and the essential first.

Essay 12📍 Covers: Ch. 5 › 5.4 Operating-system security settings as risk factors + 5.5 Risk factors by resource type and internal information sources

Least privilege means that every account, person or program receives only the rights its task requires, and no more. Daily work (mail, browsing, documents) is done with a standard account; elevation is used only for a specific administrative action.

The first setting is account privileges. In both cases the vulnerability is organizational as well as technical: nobody decided who really needs which right. Removing local administrator rights and setting share permissions by role limits the damage of one click.

A qualitative assessment rates likelihood and impact with defined categories (Kalmia's five levels from rare to almost certain and from negligible to severe) and reads the level in a matrix. Its strength is speed: it works without financial data and it can include harms that are hard to price, such as safety or reputation. Its limit is subjectivity, together with the fact that ordinal scores hide differences: a 10 and a 16 are both HIGH, and 16 is not "twice" 8.

A quantitative assessment puts money and frequency on the risk: SLE (single loss expectancy) = asset value × exposure factor, and ALE (annualized loss expectancy) = SLE × ARO (annualized rate of occurrence). Its strength is that it answers the manager's question "is this control worth its price?" by comparing the ALE reduction with the control's annualized cost. Its limit is that reliable numbers are hard to find in a small company.

Kalmia rates all twelve risks qualitatively, which keeps the register fast and readable, and adds an ALE for its top risks, where the cost-benefit decisions are made.

Essay 14📍 Covers: Ch. 6 › 6.2 Effects on CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability), complexity of exploitation and spread to other systems + 6.3 The organization's ability to respond to an incident

Explain how the complexity of exploitation, the possibility of spread and the ability to respond each influence a risk rating. (8–12 lines)

💡 Hint: Decide, for each factor, whether it moves the likelihood or the impact.

✅ Model answer

The complexity of exploitation mainly affects the likelihood. If a public exploit exists, works remotely, needs no account and no user action, and the vulnerability is listed as exploited in the KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) catalog, success is easy and probable. If it needs physical access, special privileges or several user actions, the likelihood drops.

The possibility of spread mainly affects the impact. A flat network, a reused administrator password or a backup server that can write everywhere let an attack move from the first machine to many others, so the consequences grow from one laptop to the whole company. Segmentation and named accounts shrink that blast radius.

The ability to respond also changes the impact, through time. An organization with a written plan, named roles, alerts and tested backups detects, contains and recovers in hours; one without them may need weeks. The expected outage is compared with the MTD (maximum tolerable downtime): when detection plus containment plus recovery exceeds it, the impact sits at the top of the scale. At Kalmia, a readiness index of eight criteria scored 0 to 3 out of 24 makes this judgment explicit.

Essay 15📍 Covers: Ch. 6 › 6.4 The risk matrix, the risk register and prioritization

Describe Kalmia's risk matrix and the limits of any risk matrix, and explain what the register adds to compensate. (8–12 lines)

💡 Hint: The matrix shows the picture; the register holds the reasons.

✅ Model answer

Kalmia's matrix crosses likelihood and impact, each from 1 to 5. The score is their product, from 1 to 25, read in four bands: 1 to 4 LOW, 5 to 9 MEDIUM, 10 to 16 HIGH and 20 to 25 CRITICAL; 17 to 19 cannot occur on this grid. Each band carries an action, from a yearly review for LOW to immediate action within seven days, decided by the President, for CRITICAL.

Every matrix has limits. The numbers are ordinal, so 16 is not twice 8. Range compression puts very different risks in the same band, and a frequent minor risk (5 × 2) gets the same score as a rare severe one (2 × 5), although they call for different treatments.

The register compensates by keeping the details visible: likelihood and impact separately, the justification and the evidence for each rating, the confidence, the owner, the treatment option, the residual level and the review date. It also applies written rules, such as Kalmia's tie-break: equal scores are ranked by the higher impact. For the top risks, an ALE (annualized loss expectancy) adds a money figure.

Essay 16📍 Covers: Ch. 7 › 7.1 Risk treatment options — reduce, transfer, avoid, accept

Describe the four risk treatment options and give a Kalmia example for each. Explain why insurance alone is not an answer to R-01. (8–12 lines)

💡 Hint: Each option changes something different: the numbers, the bill, the activity, or nothing but a signed decision.

✅ Model answer

Reduce means adding or improving controls that lower the likelihood or the impact; at Kalmia, MFA (multi-factor authentication) on all 31 Microsoft 365 accounts reduces account takeover. Transfer means sharing part of the consequences with someone else, usually an insurer or a contractual partner; a cyber insurance policy that pays recovery costs is transfer. Avoid means stopping the activity that creates the risk; deleting old loyalty exports that nobody uses, and no longer producing them, avoids the risk of their theft. Accept means keeping the risk knowingly, with a written decision by the authorized person; a LOW risk kept with a yearly review is accepted.

Insurance alone does not answer R-01 because it does not change the likelihood of the attack or the length of the outage. The files stay encrypted, wholesale preparation stops, the stolen copy stays with the criminals and customers' trust suffers; the insurer only pays part of the bill, often on condition that basic controls such as backups and MFA were in place. Insurance can complete a reduction plan, never replace it.

Essay 17📍 Covers: Ch. 7 › 7.2 Types of controls and matching controls to risks + 7.3 Applicability by risk level and cost versus benefit

Show that Kalmia's plan for R-01 respects the applicability rule, classifying each control by category and function. Then explain how cost versus benefit is calculated for one control. (8–12 lines)

💡 Hint: A CRITICAL risk needs three functions. Then: annualized cost, ALE (annualized loss expectancy) before and after, net benefit, ROSI (return on security investment).

✅ Model answer

The applicability rule says that a CRITICAL risk needs controls covering three functions, a HIGH risk two and a MEDIUM risk one. R-01 is CRITICAL, with a score of 20.

The plan contains CT-09, segmentation (technical, preventive: it limits how far the malware can go); CT-10, named administrator accounts for NAS-01 (technical with an administrative side, preventive); CT-08, an alert on mass file changes (technical, detective); and CT-07, an off-site offline backup with a monthly restore test (technical, corrective). Preventive, detective and corrective are all present, so the rule is met. CT-11, awareness training (administrative, preventive), adds a layer at the first stage.

Cost versus benefit for one control uses four steps. The annualized cost is the one-time cost divided by the useful life, plus the yearly cost. The ALE (annualized loss expectancy) is computed before and after the control. The net benefit is the ALE reduction minus the annualized cost. The ROSI (return on security investment) is the net benefit divided by the annualized cost; a positive value means the control saves more than it costs.

Essay 18📍 Covers: Ch. 7 › 7.4 Continuity — backups, redundancy, BCP (business continuity plan) and DRP (disaster recovery plan) + 7.5 Residual risk and risk acceptance

Compare a backup, redundancy, a BCP (business continuity plan) and a DRP (disaster recovery plan), and explain why Kalmia's old USB (Universal Serial Bus) disk was "a copy, not a backup you can count on". (8–12 lines)

💡 Hint: Data, service, business process, IT (information technology) restoration; then check the old disk against 3-2-1.

✅ Model answer

A backup is a separate copy of the data that can be restored after loss or encryption. Redundancy duplicates components (two disks in a mirror, a second Internet link, a UPS (uninterruptible power supply)) so that the service survives a failure; it is not a backup, because a mirror copies deletions and encryption instantly. The BCP describes how the business keeps its critical processes running during the disruption, with workarounds such as manual café sales. The DRP describes how IT restores its systems: who declares the disaster, the order of recovery, the contacts and procedures.

Kalmia's old arrangement was one USB disk permanently attached to NAS-01, copied weekly and never tested. Against the 3-2-1 rule (three copies, two media, one off-site, and that copy offline or immutable), it failed: it was on site, online and reachable by the same ransomware, so it would be encrypted with the server. A weekly copy meant an RPO (recovery point objective) of up to seven days, and without a test restore the RTO (recovery time objective) was unknown. CT-07 replaces it with a nightly off-site, offline copy and a monthly restore test.

Essay 19📍 Covers: Ch. 8 › 8.1 The standard response to malware — isolate, identify, contain, eradicate, recover, learn

Describe the six steps of the standard malware response and explain the two most tempting mistakes of the first hour. (8–12 lines)

💡 Hint: One mistake destroys evidence; the other destroys the last copy.

✅ Model answer

A standard response is written before the incident, with a role for each step. Isolate: disconnect the affected machines from the network and keep them powered on. Identify: establish the family, the hosts, the time and the vector from the antivirus or EDR (endpoint detection and response) console, the note, file names, the logs and the mail gateway. Contain: disable compromised accounts, change the shared administrator password from a clean machine, block the C2 (command-and-control) address, take shares offline and preserve evidence on labelled media. Eradicate: remove persistence and, for rootkits and ransomware, wipe and reinstall from known-good media, then close the vector. Recover: restore from a clean, offline, tested backup in business priority and monitor for weeks. Learn: write the report with timeline, root cause, cost and changes, and notify the parties required by law and contracts.

The first tempting mistake is to power the machines off "to stop the damage": disconnecting stops the spread just as well, while a shutdown destroys volatile evidence and may trigger a second stage. The second is to reconnect the backup disk "to see if it works": if it was attached during the encryption, it is encrypted too, and plugging it into an infected system can destroy what survived.
